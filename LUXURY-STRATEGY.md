# CASWISS
## Quiet luxury: strategy, rationale and launch plan

**Prepared for:** CASWISS, Accra  
**Scope:** The 2026 digital flagship and private-client experience  
**Date:** November 2026

---

## 1. Executive summary

CASWISS does not need to look like a bigger shop. It needs to feel like a more considered house.

The new site is built around one strategic idea: **the website should behave like the first five minutes of a private viewing.** It should lower the voice, narrow the choice and make the next step feel personal rather than transactional.

That idea is expressed through three jobs:

1. **Signal taste before price.** A restrained editorial design, real inventory photography and disciplined copy establish that CASWISS has a point of view.
2. **Give enough detail to create confidence.** Every piece has a name, reference, materials, condition note and delivery path. The site is reserved about price, not vague about the product.
3. **Route interest to a human advisor.** WhatsApp and the private-viewing form replace the cart. The site creates a qualified conversation; the advisor closes it.

The commercial bet is straightforward: **fewer, better pieces; fewer, better enquiries; stronger relationships and pricing power over time.** The site is designed to make the right client want to start a conversation, not to make every visitor add something to a basket.

### What has changed

| | **Before** | **Now** |
|---|---|---|
| **Offer** | Watches, presented as a broad catalogue | Timepieces and leather, presented as one curated wardrobe |
| **Imagery** | Generic stock photography | The actual pieces held and sold by CASWISS |
| **Feeling** | Transactional, mass-market | Editorial, private, unhurried |
| **Language** | Product listings and sales copy | Provenance, material, condition and point of view |
| **Conversion** | Add to cart / generic contact | WhatsApp advisor, product enquiry, private viewing |
| **Price** | Displayed like a commodity | Confirmed on enquiry, after a human conversation |
| **Service** | A contact detail | A named advisor from question to delivery |

The rest of this document explains the research behind those choices, how the design system works, how to brief future photography, and how to bring both the CASWISS team and CASWISS clients along with the new direction.

## 2. The strategic idea

### 2.1 From shop to house

Most watch and shoe sites compete on inventory count, discounting and speed. That is a race CASWISS should not enter. The stronger position is **curator**: a smaller selection, chosen by a person with taste, explained with honesty and delivered with care.

The line **“Quietly extraordinary”** is the organising idea:

- **Quiet** is the manner: restrained type, a narrow palette, generous space, no shouting.
- **Extraordinary** is the substance: the dial, the leather, the provenance, the service.
- The contrast between the two is the brand.

### 2.2 Why timepieces *and* leather

Watches alone make CASWISS a specialist shop. Watches and leather make it a **way of dressing**. The two categories share the same buyer, the same values and the same occasions:

- a watch and a loafer for a meeting;
- a diver and a boat shoe for the weekend;
- a gold rectangle and a dark trouser for the evening.

The pairing gives CASWISS a reason to be considered for more of a client’s wardrobe, a higher average basket and a more frequent reason to return. It also makes the brand feel like a house, not a category.

### 2.3 Who this is for

These are working assumptions to validate through the first twenty client conversations, not fixed personas.

| Audience | What they need | What convinces them | Role of the site |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accra’s quietly confident professionals** | A small edit of pieces that work across work and weekend | Material quality, condition, a discreet look, a person who knows the stock | Make browsing feel like an invitation; make asking easy |
| **The diaspora client and gift-giver** | Trust at a distance, clear next steps, reliable delivery | Real images, specific details, named advisor, delivery path | Replace uncertainty with a conversation; make WhatsApp the bridge |
| **The watch-curious or new collector** | Orientation without condescension | Explanations in plain language, scale and context, honest condition notes | Educate without overwhelming; offer a private viewing |
| **The existing client** | First look, convenience, continuity | Knowing the advisor, recognition, early access | Give the adviser a link to send; make re-entry simple |

### 2.4 Positioning statement

> For discerning clients in Accra and the diaspora, CASWISS is an independent curator of timepieces and leather. We select fewer pieces, document them honestly and serve each client personally. The result is a wardrobe of objects chosen for how they are made, how they wear and how they make the wearer feel.

### 2.5 The luxury paradox we designed for

Luxury clients want two things that appear to conflict:

- **Reserve:** they do not want to be sold to, rushed or treated like a mass-market shopper.
- **Reassurance:** they still want photographs, measurements, materials, condition, delivery and a clear way to ask.

The answer is not to be stingy with information. It is to be **generous with detail and reserved about pressure.** The site gives the client the evidence to feel confident, then offers a private conversation rather than a checkout.

## 3. What the research says

### 3.1 Atmosphere changes what a site is worth

A study of luxury homepage design found that luxury homepages differ from conventional ones in four consistent ways: darker backgrounds, a larger or full-screen stage for the main image, horizontal navigation and a substantial reduction in elements. In a laboratory experiment, that combination significantly affected perceptions of conspicuousness and uniqueness. [1]

**Design implication:** use the dark ink and ivory palette, a full-bleed hero, a quiet horizontal navigation and far fewer elements than a conventional e-commerce site. Do not fill the page to prove the inventory is large; the restraint is doing strategic work.

### 3.2 Luxury is researched online, even when it is bought in person

Nielsen Norman Group’s research on luxury e-commerce found that luxury shoppers use digital channels heavily during consideration, even when they intend to buy in a boutique. Their frustration is rarely with the absence of a cart; it is with thin product details: missing measurements, unexplained jargon, too few photographs and no sense of scale or styling. NN/g recommends that brands that withhold price still provide a price **range** where possible, and that they use the website to support the advisor relationship rather than replace it. [2]

**Design implication:** the product detail dialog includes reference, materials, condition, delivery and a direct advisor link. Price is withheld, but the product is not. The advisor is available at every decision point.

### 3.3 The product must be shown in context

Baymard’s luxury-goods and jewellery-and-watches research consistently finds that brand presentation must coexist with practical shopping tasks: navigation, filtering, detailed product information and imagery that shows wear, scale and context. Luxury sites often look beautiful while making it hard to evaluate the item. [3]

**Design implication:** a clean grid and filters make the edit navigable; white-background studio shots are paired with editorial and on-body images; cards carry enough specification to distinguish pieces without turning the page into a spreadsheet.

### 3.4 Quiet branding is not the absence of branding

Han, Nunes and Drèze introduced the concept of **brand prominence**: the conspicuousness of a brand’s mark on a product. They found that wealthy consumers who are low in need for status prefer quiet goods that only their peers recognise, while consumers high in status need prefer louder marks. The researchers argue that quiet luxury is a real signalling strategy, not merely a minimalist aesthetic. [4]

**Design implication:** CASWISS should not lead with a wall of third-party logos. It should build its own **griffe** – recognisable codes and details. The house name, the restrained gold rule, the editorial captions and the calibre of the photography are the signals. Brand names remain visible on the pieces and in the product information, but the *house* is the frame.

### 3.5 Service is the product, not a support channel

NN/g’s luxury research emphasises deep personal connection: digital tools should enhance the customer–salesperson relationship, not replace it. [2] The same body of research warns that attention to detail separates luxury from its imitation: typos, broken links and slow pages cheapen the brand faster than a conservative design ever could. [2]

**Design implication:** the WhatsApp path is not a fallback. It is the conversion layer. The site’s job is to make that first message feel natural, specific and low-friction.

### 3.6 Scarcity must be true

Scarcity and social proof can increase desire, but the research also shows they can backfire, especially with sophisticated buyers who recognise persuasion tactics. [5] For a curator whose reputation depends on trust, fake urgency is not worth the risk.

**Design implication:** no countdown timers, no “only 2 left” theatre, no invented waiting lists. The edit is small because it was chosen carefully, not because the site is manufacturing panic.

### 3.7 Getting buy-in is a design problem too

Research on presenting UX work to stakeholders recommends grounding proposals in evidence, using interactive prototypes rather than static mockups, showing before/after impact, anticipating objections and framing the work around business outcomes. [6]

**Design implication:** this document and the prototype walkthrough are part of the deliverable. The client should experience the site as a client would, then see how each decision connects to a business outcome. That is how the direction survives internal debate.

## 4. From research to interface

The design system is deliberately small. Every element earns its place.

| Design decision | What it looks like | Why it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| **Ink and paper palette** | Deep blue-black, warm ivory, brushed gold used as an accent | Dark stage + light editorial space; the gold reads as material, not decoration |
| **Cormorant Garamond + Jost** | Large, high-contrast serif for emotion; quiet geometric sans for information | The serif carries desire and editorial authority; the sans keeps product facts legible |
| **Full-bleed hero** | A real still life of a blue-dial watch; copy sits in its own ivory field | Gives the product a stage and the words room, without a busy split-screen of equals |
| **Horizontal navigation** | Three links left, wordmark centre, two actions right | Matches the luxury-homepage pattern and keeps the header calm |
| **Generous whitespace** | Long vertical rhythm, fewer borders, no sidebars | Space is a luxury signal; it also lets the photography breathe |
| **Full control over imagery** | Own inventory, consistent crops, `object-fit: contain` on warm paper | Avoids the harsh white boxes of marketplace listings; keeps the product truthful |
| **Product cards** | Brand, name, specification, “Price on request”, quiet “View piece” reveal | Enough to choose; not so much that the grid becomes a spreadsheet |
| **Filters** | All / Timepieces / Footwear with counts | Practical navigation inside the edit; the counts reinforce curation |
| **Product dialog** | Large image, second detail image, description, reference, materials, condition, delivery, WhatsApp | Deep information without a separate page; keeps the browsing spell intact |
| **Price on request** | No public price; “Confirmed on enquiry” beside the edit | Preserves exclusivity and starts a conversation; detail elsewhere prevents frustration |
| **WhatsApp advisor** | Prefilled, piece-specific message from every product path | Meets the client where they already are; removes form friction |
| **Private viewing form** | Name, phone, interest, preferred date; opens WhatsApp with a structured message | Converts intent into a specific, human request |
| **Named advisor** | A real person in the header, footer and floating contact | Makes the house feel accountable, not anonymous |
| **Restrained motion** | Short reveals, soft dialog entry, reduced-motion support | Adds polish; never makes the client wait for the interface to perform |
| **Mobile-first discipline** | Single-column rhythm, full-screen mobile menu, fixed close control in the dialog | Most of the audience arrives on a phone, often from a message or social link |
| **Honest microcopy** | No hype, no false urgency, no payment details collected | Protects trust and reduces the fear of being trapped in a sales funnel |

### 4.1 The reading sequence

1. **Arrive:** the hero establishes the house, the edit and the private-viewing invitation.
2. **Believe:** the four-part standard – Curated, Documented, Personal, Delivered – answers the question “why here?”
3. **Browse:** the edit gives a manageable choice, with filters for the two categories.
4. **Inspect:** the dialog supplies the details a serious buyer needs.
5. **Ask:** WhatsApp opens with the piece already named; the appointment form opens with a structured request.
6. **Meet:** the private viewing happens in Accra, or through a scheduled video conversation for diaspora clients.

### 4.2 What we deliberately did not do

- **No cart or checkout.** For a curated, low-volume, high-touch offer, a checkout would create more abandonment than it removes. The 90-day review can revisit this decision with data.
- **No public pricing table.** Price-on-request is defensible only because the rest of the product information is generous. If response times slip or price questions dominate, add ranges.
- **No stock photography.** One of the strongest assets CASWISS has is that the pieces are real. Generic imagery would erase that advantage.
- **No logo wall.** The third-party brands provide recognition, but the selection and the service are the CASWISS product.
- **No countdown timers, fake reviews or “only 2 left”.** Sophisticated buyers detect manufactured urgency, and it contradicts the idea of a curator.
- **No newsletter pop-up on arrival.** A private viewing should not begin with an interception.
- **No dense mega-menu.** Three sections and a client-care path are enough. The edit is the navigation.

The original photography was supplied as phone images. The best frames were selected, cropped and optimised into WebP and JPEG variants for large and small screens. White-background product shots are placed on warm paper with `mix-blend-mode: multiply`, so they sit inside the page rather than glaring out of it.

This is a strong first edit because the pieces are real. It is not yet a finished photographic system because the source images vary in light, angle and styling. The next shoot is the single highest-leverage brand investment after launch.

### 5.2 The five-shot standard for every product

Every future piece should have:

1. **Hero still life (4:5).** The piece on warm paper, stone, linen or brushed steel, with room for copy. This is the image that could lead a campaign.
2. **Product front (4:5 or 1:1).** Clean, full silhouette, no crop. This is the image the client inspects.
3. **Three-quarter or angled view.** Shows depth, profile and how the light moves across the material.
4. **Macro detail.** Dial, crown, stitching, welt, buckle, leather grain. This is where craft becomes visible.
5. **In context.** On a wrist or foot, with a cuff, trouser or hand, in natural light. This gives scale and desire.

For watches, add a **caseback or movement** image when relevant. For shoes, add a **pair-and-single** composition and a **sole** view. If a piece has a condition note, photograph the condition honestly.

### 5.3 Art direction rules

- **Light:** one broad, soft source; gentle shadow; no hard ring-light flash. Natural window light is the house look.
- **Colour:** warm neutral. Keep ivory, stone, walnut and brushed steel as the recurring set. Avoid saturated props and strong colour casts.
- **Composition:** quiet, asymmetrical, editorial. Leave negative space for type. Do not centre every object as if it were a catalogue cut-out.
- **Props:** leather gloves, a watchmaker’s loupe, a linen cloth, a stone plinth, a worn book. Props should suggest care and knowledge, not costume.
- **People:** real clients or local models with a natural, unforced posture. Neutral clothing, no competing logos. Hands and shoes must be immaculate.
- **Retouching:** remove dust and distractions; preserve metal, grain, patina and honest wear. Never retouch out a flaw a client would discover in person.
- **No filters that lie.** If the dial is deep blue, it should be deep blue in the hand as well.

### 5.4 Shoot brief for the next production

| Deliverable | Ratio | Minimum size | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Campaign still life | 4:5 | 1600 × 2000 | Homepage hero, social, invitations |
| Product front | 4:5 | 1600 × 2000 | Product grid and dialog |
| Product angle | 4:5 | 1600 × 2000 | Dialog |
| Detail | 1:1 | 1600 × 1600 | Dialog, social close-ups |
| On-body / in context | 4:5 or 3:2 | 2000 px wide | Editorial, collections, product dialog |
| Wide editorial | 3:2 | 2400 px wide | Collection cards, quote bands, campaign |

**File handling:** export WebP and JPEG, names in lowercase with hyphens, width-specific suffixes (`-small`, `-large`), and a companion spreadsheet with alt text, product reference, permission notes and photographer credit.

**Production cadence:** one editorial story per month, three to five new pieces per quarter, and a seasonal refresh of the hero and collection images. A small, consistent library beats a large, inconsistent one.

### 5.5 Image integrity

CASWISS is selling trust as much as product. That means:

- Do not use an image of a different piece or colourway.
- Do not use a “representative” photo when the actual item is available to photograph.
- Disclose condition in both words and images.
- Keep a record of the source and permissions for every image.
- Treat brand names and logos with respect; confirm that the business is authorised to sell each brand and that the footer disclaimer remains accurate.

## 6. The client experience

### 6.1 First-time visitor

1. Lands on a purposeful hero with a clear point of view.
2. Sees the four-part standard and understands the service model.
3. Browses a finite edit, filters by category, opens two or three pieces.
4. Reads specific materials and condition notes, not generic sales copy.
5. Sends a piece-specific WhatsApp message or requests a viewing.
6. Receives a reply from a named advisor who already knows what they looked at.

### 6.2 Returning client

1. Uses the header or a saved link to return to the edit.
2. Filters directly to timepieces or footwear.
3. Asks the advisor for a piece not shown, or for the full catalogue.
4. Books a viewing, or asks for a video walkthrough if abroad.

### 6.3 The advisor response standard

The website can create the impression; the response must keep it. Recommended service levels:

- **WhatsApp during opening hours:** acknowledge within one business hour; full answer within the same day where possible.
- **After hours:** acknowledge the next working morning.
- **Appointment requests:** confirm or offer alternatives within 24 hours.
- **Before payment:** share a condition report, provenance notes, delivery timing and the returns position in writing.
- **After delivery:** a personal follow-up within one week. Ask how the piece wears; do not immediately sell the next one.

### 6.4 What “price on request” must mean

Price on request is not an excuse to be slow or evasive. It should mean:

- the advisor responds with a specific price, not a range if a specific price exists;
- the client can ask about payment, delivery and returns without friction;
- the advisor is honest about what is fixed and what is negotiable;
- the house keeps an internal price list so every advisor gives the same answer.

If enquiry volume shows that price opacity is costing qualified buyers, the next iteration should test **price bands** (“From GH₵…”) on selected pieces rather than revealing everything at once. That preserves the private-client feel while reducing uncertainty.

## 7. Bringing the client on board

The goal is not simply approval. It is **ownership**: the CASWISS team should be able to explain the direction, defend it and operate it without the designer in the room.

### 7.1 The five-slide narrative for the client meeting

1. **The shift.** “We are not building a bigger shop. We are building a more considered house.”
2. **The evidence.** Show the four research principles: atmosphere, detail, quiet signalling, personal service.
3. **The experience.** Walk through the site on a phone, then a laptop, using a real piece. Do not show static screens only.
4. **The proof.** Show the actual inventory, the conditions disclosed, the WhatsApp message that arrives with the piece already named.
5. **The plan.** 90 days, five metrics, a decision gate and a clear division of labour.

### 7.2 Workshop 1 — Positioning and language (90 minutes)

**Participants:** owner, brand lead, lead advisor, photographer or content lead.  
**Pre-work:** each participant brings five pieces they would put in the window and one sentence describing the client they most want.

| Time | Exercise | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 10 min | Why now: the commercial case for repositioning | Shared problem statement |
| 25 min | The evidence: atmosphere, detail, quiet luxury, service | Agreement on the principles that matter |
| 25 min | Reactions to the prototype: what feels like us, what does not? | A list of decisions, not preferences |
| 20 min | Words we own / words we avoid | Approved language bank |
| 10 min | Price policy, service levels, next steps | Named owners and dates |

**Output:** approved positioning, language bank, price policy, service standards and photography priorities.

### 7.3 Workshop 2 — Prototype walkthrough (60–75 minutes)

Ask each participant to complete five tasks without help:

1. Find a timepiece that would work with tailoring.
2. Understand what “price on request” means and how to ask.
3. Open a piece and send a specific enquiry.
4. Request a private viewing for a particular date.
5. Find the condition and delivery information.

Then ask three questions:

- What would make you **distrust** this page?
- What would a **good advisor** say at this point?
- What is missing that a serious buyer would need before arranging a viewing?

Capture **decisions** and **blockers**, not opinions about typefaces. Separate “brand feedback” (owner) from “usability feedback” (client) and “technical feedback” (developer).

### 7.4 Workshop 3 — Operations (90 minutes)

The site is only as luxurious as the operation behind it. Cover:

- **WhatsApp:** one named advisor, saved replies, handover, privacy, after-hours expectations.
- **Photography:** the five-shot standard, retouching rules, approval, file naming, alt text.
- **Inventory:** what happens when a piece sells; remove it or mark it as reserved; never leave a dead enquiry path.
- **Appointments:** calendar, confirmation, address, what the client should bring, follow-up.
- **Content:** one editorial story a month; three social posts a week; a quarterly review of the edit.
- **Measurement:** the dashboard, the weekly review and the 90-day decision gate.

### 7.5 Stakeholder map and decision rights

| Decision | Recommends | Decides | Consulted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand positioning and language | Design lead | Owner | Lead advisor |
| Product selection and prices | Owner / buying | Owner | Lead advisor |
| Photography art direction | Design lead / photographer | Owner | Lead advisor |
| Website scope and sequence | Design lead | Owner | Developer |
| WhatsApp service standards | Lead advisor | Owner | Design lead |
| Launch date and client invitations | Design lead | Owner | Lead advisor |

The important discipline: **one decider per decision.** A luxury brand cannot be designed by committee, but it can be informed by everyone.

### 7.6 Objection handling

| Objection | What is really being asked | Response |
|---|---|---|
| “Without prices, we will lose sales.” | “Will this pay for itself?” | The site is designed to create qualified conversations, not anonymous carts. NN/g found that missing product detail is a bigger frustration than missing price; price ranges can be tested after launch. Track enquiries, response times and close rate for 90 days. |
| “It is too dark / too empty.” | “Will clients feel welcome?” | The darkness is a stage, not a maze. Walk the client through the content on a phone. Whitespace is doing the luxury work. Test comprehension, not taste. |
| “We need a checkout now.” | “We need revenue now.” | The current offer is low-volume and high-touch; a checkout would add friction and cost without solving trust. Use the 90-day gate. If the data shows repeatable, price-known demand, add a reserve-and-pay option. |
| “Our clients are on WhatsApp, not websites.” | “Why invest in a site?” | The website is the credible catalogue that makes the WhatsApp conversation easier. It works after hours, for the diaspora and for search. WhatsApp remains the CTA. |
| “Our competitor looks brighter and busier.” | “Are we falling behind?” | They are optimising for a different client and a different margin. Benchmark against luxury houses, not marketplaces. The goal is pricing power, not raw traffic. |
| “The phone photos were fine.” | “Can we avoid a shoot?” | The current photos prove the inventory is real, but they vary in light and context. A consistent shoot is the fastest way to raise perceived value across the whole edit. |
| “Luxury means gold and serif type.” | “How will people know it is luxury?” | Gold and serif type are not enough; they are costume without substance. The signals are the selection, the detail, the condition notes and the service. |

### 7.7 Language bank

**Use**

- “A private edit”
- “Chosen with intent”
- “Quietly extraordinary”
- “We tell you everything we know”
- “Price confirmed on enquiry”
- “By appointment, in Accra”
- “A named advisor”
- “Across Ghana and worldwide on request”
- “Available for inspection”

**Avoid**

- “Huge sale”, “must-have”, “while stocks last”
- “Affordable luxury”
- “Exclusive deal”
- “100% authentic!!”
- “Limited time only”
- “Luxury lifestyle”
- Excessive exclamation marks and emoji in public copy
- Vague claims such as “best quality” without evidence

**Tone:** first person plural, short sentences, specific nouns, no pressure. The advisor can be warmer and more conversational in WhatsApp than the site is in public.

### 7.8 The one-page leave-behind

If the client needs something to circulate internally, give them this:

> CASWISS is moving from a watch catalogue to a curated house for timepieces and leather. The site uses real inventory, an editorial point of view and a private-advisor path instead of a cart. The immediate goal is not to maximise clicks; it is to increase the number and quality of private conversations. Success will be judged on qualified enquiries, appointments, response times, close rate and client perception over 90 days. The direction is research-led and can be tested without abandoning the brand.

## 8. Bringing customers on board

### 8.1 The first invitation

Clients should not arrive at a launch. They should receive an invitation. A short, personal message from the advisor is more powerful than a mass campaign.

**Suggested WhatsApp / email copy**

> Good afternoon [Name],
>
> I hope you are well. We have just finished a small digital edit of the pieces we are holding in Accra, and I wanted you to have a first look before we share it more widely: [link].
>
> If anything catches your eye, simply reply here. I can send you the full details, a condition report or a short video, and we can arrange a quiet viewing if you would like to see it in person.
>
> — [Advisor], CASWISS

Rules: send it personally, use the client’s name, do not include a discount, do not send it to a list in which the recipient can see other names, and follow up once only.

### 8.2 What the site must explain before the client asks

- **Price:** “Price on request · confirmed on enquiry.” The product dialog repeats that no payment details are collected.
- **What happens next:** WhatsApp opens with a specific message; the advisor confirms availability, price and condition.
- **Where:** Accra, by appointment.
- **Delivery:** across Ghana and worldwide on request.
- **Risk:** the footer states that brand names belong to their owners; the advisor shares the condition and returns position in writing before payment.

If a client still asks “How much?”, the answer should be a price, not a policy.

### 8.3 The first 30 days with clients

1. **Invite the top 30 existing clients personally.** Ask for a reply, not a click.
2. **Ask five of them to browse while you watch.** Note where they hesitate.
3. **Run two private viewings** using the site as the visual aid.
4. **Publish three social posts a week:** one piece, one detail, one point of view. No daily discounting.
5. **Capture one editorial story:** a client, an advisor, a piece being prepared, or a close study of a material.
6. **Review the WhatsApp questions.** The most common question is the next content brief.

### 8.4 In-store and diaspora

- Keep the site open on a tablet in the appointment room. The advisor can show the full detail, condition and delivery terms without printing a catalogue.
- Put a small card in the viewing room and on packaging: a QR code to the edit, the advisor’s WhatsApp and the private-viewing link.
- For diaspora clients, offer a scheduled video viewing, mention time zones and provide a condition report before payment. The digital experience should feel as considered as the room in Accra.

### 8.5 The feedback loop

After the first five conversations, ask:

1. What made you decide to make contact?
2. What did you want to know that the site did not tell you?
3. What almost stopped you?
4. How did the WhatsApp response feel?
5. What would you tell a friend this house is for?

Do not ask “Do you like the design?” It produces noise. Ask what they understood, what they trusted and what they did next.

## 9. 90-day rollout plan

| Phase | Timing | Actions | Owner | Success measure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Prepare** | Week 0 | Final copy and claims sign-off; domain and analytics; WhatsApp Business profile; named advisor; condition/returns/delivery policy confirmed; accessibility and mobile QA | Owner + design + developer | Site ready; all claims defensible; response owner named |
| **1. Soft launch** | Weeks 1–2 | Personal invitations to top 30 clients; staff training and role-play; baseline metrics captured; five client observation sessions | Owner + lead advisor | 20 personal invitations sent; 5 real conversations; response-time baseline |
| **2. Public launch** | Weeks 3–6 | Social and search launch; two private viewings; weekly analytics review; first content story; first two A/B tests (hero copy, price-band treatment) | Design + owner + advisor | Qualified enquiries; appointments; dialog opens; no drop-off errors |
| **3. Refine** | Weeks 7–12 | Second photo shoot; update top 5 products; add policy pages; test price ranges if warranted; review objection log; prepare checkout decision | Owner + photographer + design | Stronger image consistency; faster responses; better close rate |
| **Decision gate** | End of week 12 | Decide: stay advisory; add reserve-and-pay; add price bands; invest in a private client portal | Owner | Decision based on evidence, not preference |

### 9.1 What must be true before public launch

- The client has signed off every claim about provenance, condition and delivery.
- The WhatsApp number is monitored by a named person, with a backup.
- The condition and returns position is written down and shareable.
- Every product image corresponds to the available piece.
- The mobile experience has been tested on a real phone, not only a simulator.
- Analytics events are working: WhatsApp click, product dialog open, filter use, appointment form submit.
- The domain in the canonical URL and social previews is the live domain.

### 9.2 The 90-day decision gate

At the end of 90 days, review four questions:

1. Are qualified conversations increasing, and are they the right conversations?
2. Are advisors able to answer within the promised time?
3. Which pieces attract attention but not enquiry? Why?
4. Is price opacity creating a barrier that ranges would solve?

Then choose one of four paths:

- **Stay advisory** if the quality of conversation is high and close rate is healthy.
- **Add price bands** if “how much?” dominates and clients drop before speaking.
- **Add reserve-and-pay** if demand is repeatable and clients want to transact without a viewing.
- **Invest in a private client room** if the same clients return and want first access, care history and appointments in one place.

## 10. Measurement

### 10.1 North-star metric

**Qualified private conversations per month.** A qualified conversation is a WhatsApp or appointment enquiry from a real prospective client about a specific piece or a genuine styling need, not a press request, supplier message or spam.

This is the right north star because the site is designed to create a relationship, not an anonymous transaction.

### 10.2 KPI framework

| Layer | Metric | Why it matters | Review cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reach** | Sessions, direct traffic, branded search | Is the house being found for the right reasons? | Weekly |
| **Interest** | Product dialog opens per session; filter usage; time on edit | Is the edit creating desire? | Weekly |
| **Intent** | WhatsApp clicks; appointment requests; catalogue requests | Are clients taking the next step? | Weekly |
| **Quality** | Enquiry-to-viewing rate; viewing-to-sale rate; response time | Is the human layer converting? | Monthly |
| **Value** | Average order value; repeat and referral rate; margin | Is the positioning creating pricing power? | Monthly |
| **Brand** | Aided/unaided luxury perception; trust; “would recommend” | Is the direction building equity, not just clicks? | Quarterly |
| **Guardrails** | Mobile bounce; form errors; broken image rate; page speed | Is the experience quietly flawless? | Weekly |

### 10.3 Instrumentation

Add privacy-respecting event tracking for:

- `product_dialog_open` (with product reference)
- `filter_apply` (with category)
- `whatsapp_click` (with source and product reference)
- `appointment_form_submit`
- `catalogue_request`
- `outbound_tel_click`

Use a first-party analytics tool or a privacy-compliant setup. Do not put client names, phone numbers or message content into analytics. The advisor can use UTM-tagged catalogue links and a simple spreadsheet to track which conversations close.

### 10.4 How to review the numbers

Hold a 30-minute weekly review:

1. What did clients do?
2. Where did they hesitate?
3. What did they ask for that we did not show?
4. What will we change this week?

Once a month, look at the commercial layer: enquiries, viewings, close rate, average value, repeat and referral. Do not let weekly click noise override the brand strategy or the monthly evidence.

## 11. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Price opacity damages trust** | “If you have to ask, you can’t afford it” is a stereotype that loses real buyers | Fast, specific answers; internal price list; test price ranges after 90 days; never make a client ask twice |
| **WhatsApp response is slow** | The site promises a private service; a slow reply breaks it | Named advisor, backup, saved replies, service-level agreement, weekly response-time review |
| **Image quality varies** | Inconsistent photography undermines the luxury signal | Five-shot standard, a single retoucher, consistent set and grading, quarterly audit |
| **Sold pieces remain live** | A dead enquiry path destroys trust and wastes advisor time | Daily inventory check; remove or mark sold; keep a “recently found homes” archive if desired |
| **Inauthentic or unverified stock** | Reputation and legal risk | Authenticity and provenance checks; written disclosure; legal review of brand relationships and returns policy |
| **Over-designed but hard to use** | Luxury sites often mistake beauty for clarity | Usability testing with five real clients; keep navigation, filters and product facts functional |
| **Too many choices** | A large, undifferentiated catalogue dilutes the curation | Keep the public edit finite; use the advisor for the full catalogue and special requests |
| **Social media drifts to discounts** | Discount language erodes the positioning | Content pillars: piece, detail, point of view; no public discounting; special offers handled privately |
| **Privacy and consent** | Client images and stories are sensitive | Written consent for testimonials and photographs; no client data in analytics; clear WhatsApp privacy note |
| **SEO and domain confusion** | A placeholder canonical reduces discoverability | Connect the real domain, update canonical and OG URLs, submit a sitemap, keep social handles consistent |
| **Accessibility gaps** | Exclusivity should never mean exclusion | Semantic headings, visible focus, sufficient contrast, keyboard-operable dialog and menu, reduced-motion support |

### 11.1 Content and claims checklist

Before launch, the owner must confirm:

- [ ] Every piece shown is available, or will be removed promptly when sold.
- [ ] The provenance and condition wording is accurate for each piece.
- [ ] The returns, exchanges and delivery position is documented.
- [ ] The business is authorised to sell each third-party brand; the footer disclaimer is legally reviewed.
- [ ] The price policy is agreed, including who can quote and what can be negotiated.
- [ ] The WhatsApp number is correct and reaches a named advisor.
- [ ] Photography permissions and model releases are on file.
- [ ] The live domain, canonical URL and social preview images are correct.
- [ ] Client testimonials, if used, have written permission.

## Appendix B — Open questions for the client

These should be answered before the public launch, not after:

1. Is every piece authenticated, and what exactly can we promise in writing?
2. Are the watches new, pre-owned, or a mix? How should condition be described publicly?

6. Does the business have authorisation to resell each third-party brand? What does the legal review say?
